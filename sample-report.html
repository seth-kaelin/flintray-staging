The buyer's first message

My wife and I just closed on our first house in Fort Collins. It's a 1990s two-story with a south-facing roof, and the inspector said the roof has maybe ten years left. The previous owners' electric bill averaged around $140 a month. We could put maybe $15,000 toward something, but we're stretched after the down payment. Can you walk me through our realistic options for solar, and whether it even makes sense this early?

The assistant's answer

Short answer: solar can make sense, but your roof timeline is the deciding factor.

Your usage

- A $140 monthly bill suggests moderate usage, so a modest system could offset much of it.

- A south-facing roof is ideal for production in Fort Collins.

Your realistic paths

- Cash or loan purchase: the best long-term value, but $15,000 may only cover part of a system.

- Lease or power agreement: low upfront cost, but smaller savings and harder to transfer when selling.

- Wait and plan: replace the roof first, then install panels as one project.

The roof issue

- Removing and reinstalling panels later can cost thousands.

- With about ten years left, ask a roofer whether replacing it sooner is cheaper overall.

Next step

- Get a roof assessment and a year of your own usage data before committing. Check current incentives too, since they change often.

The buyer's second message

A few things worry me. We might have kids in the next couple of years, so our usage could climb. We don't know how long we'll stay, maybe seven to ten years. I also don't want to put panels on a roof that needs replacing soon. What should I actually compare between quotes so I don't get locked into something that hurts us if we sell?

The assistant's answer

What to compare between quotes

Pricing

- Cost per watt, not just the total price.

- Estimated annual production and the assumptions behind it.

Ownership and resale

- Owned systems usually add appeal at sale. With a lease, the buyer has to take over the contract.

- Ask for the exact transfer process, fees, and buyer credit requirements.

- Check loans for prepayment penalties.

Roof coordination

- The cost to remove and reinstall panels if the roof needs work.

- Whether roof penetrations are covered by the warranty.

Growth

- Can panels be added later without replacing the inverter?

- Is the system battery-ready?

Warranties

- Panel, inverter, and workmanship terms, and who honors them if the installer closes.

Red flag

- Escalator clauses that raise payments every year can make a home harder to sell.

The buyer's first message

My wife and I just closed on our first house in Fort Collins. It's a 1990s two-story with a south-facing roof, and the inspector said the roof has maybe ten years left. The previous owners' electric bill averaged around $140 a month. We could put maybe $15,000 toward something, but we're stretched after the down payment. Can you walk me through our realistic options for solar, and whether it even makes sense this early?

The assistant's answer

Congratulations on the house. Solar can make sense for you, but I'd be cautious about rushing, mainly because of the roof.

A $140 monthly bill points to moderate usage, and a south-facing roof in Fort Collins is close to ideal. A system sized to your needs would likely cost more than $15,000 to buy outright. That means you'd probably be looking at a loan, a partial cash purchase, or a lease or power agreement.

The bigger question is timing. Panels last 25 years or more, and your roof has perhaps ten. Taking panels off and putting them back later typically costs thousands. One reasonable path is to wait, collect a year of your own usage data, and pair solar with a roof replacement in a few years. Another is to ask roofers whether replacing the roof sooner is cost-effective. Incentives exist but change, so verify the current ones.

The buyer's second message

A few things worry me. We might have kids in the next couple of years, so our usage could climb. We don't know how long we'll stay, maybe seven to ten years. I also don't want to put panels on a roof that needs replacing soon. What should I actually compare between quotes so I don't get locked into something that hurts us if we sell?

The assistant's answer

Those are the right worries, and they point toward a few specific comparisons.

On resale, ownership structure matters most. A system you own outright or through a loan generally transfers cleanly with the house. A lease or power agreement requires the buyer to take over the contract. That can complicate a sale, especially if payments rise each year under an escalator clause. Ask any lender about prepayment penalties.

On the roof, compare what each installer charges to remove and reinstall panels. Also check whether their workmanship warranty covers roof penetrations.

On growth, ask whether the inverter can handle added panels later and whether the system is battery-ready. Sizing slightly above current usage can be sensible, but oversizing based on guesses rarely pays.

Finally, compare cost per watt, projected production, and warranty terms. Ask who honors the warranties if the company closes.

Ask for everything in writing so the quotes are comparable.
